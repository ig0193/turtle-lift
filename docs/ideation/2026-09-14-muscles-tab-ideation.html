Codebase Context

The app has three L0 tabs — Workout, Muscles, Profile/History. The Workout tab is largely built; the Muscles tab root is a stub carrying placeholder text. docs/03-muscle-groups-tab-userflow.md already specifies six screens for this tab (landing, muscle group selection, sub-muscle group selection, exercise list, exercise detail, exercise history) and frames it as “exploration and reference… the app’s MuscleWiki experience — no logging happens here.”

What is already built and ready to use

Four traced body SVGs (male/female × front/back), a taxonomy of 12 parent groups / 19 sub-groups / 42 segments, and body_paths.dart holding every segment as a point list with a toPath() helper — rendered with CustomPainter, filled per segment, hit-tested with Path.contains. 260 exercises in exercises.json, each with primary/secondary arrays of parent/sub ids, an equipment value (8 distinct), a loadType (weighted 196, bodyweight 47, assisted 3, timed 14), and four fields of reviewed prose. Screen construction conventions are settled: AppScreen.root / .pushed with a pinned app bar, screenScrollPadding, Riverpod for derived values, Drift for storage.

Pain points and gaps

Session, SessionExercise and SetEntry tables do not exist yet — app_database.dart holds only a Settings table. Every personal-history surface in this tab depends on that layer landing first. exercise_search_screen.dart is a placeholder with a TODO, so the search and ranking rules are genuinely unwritten. The custom palette is not applied yet. Two shipped asset gaps are recorded in docs/00 §13: no side-delt artwork, and triceps not split by head.

The contradiction inside the spec

docs/03 lists “Muscle group selection” and “Sub-muscle group selection” as two separate screens, while the same document (line 104) states that tapping a segment “should go directly into that sub-muscle group’s exercise list, not into a generic parent-muscle view first.” Both statements cannot hold for a diagram tap. This was verified directly against the file, and it shapes the top-ranked idea below.

Constraints the ideas were qualified against

Nothing is stored that can be derived (streak, counts, PBs, per-session PB count, calorie estimate, the trained tick, the resolved title). performedOn is a local calendar date, not a timestamp. No SVG rendering package. The app bar is pinned on every screen. #D85A30 means “active / this has been worked” everywhere and #C4553A is destructive-only. The four reviewed prose fields carry injury risk and must not be rewritten. Out of scope for V1: login, cloud sync, soft delete, rest timer, workout duration, notifications, wearables, AI, premium tier, ads, multi-day splits, per-exercise illustrations, achievements/badges/level-ups.

External context

Prior art converges on one dominant pattern: sets per muscle group per week, painted on a body, with a calendar-week default and a tap that opens a per-muscle breakdown (Hevy is the reference implementation). A second, distinct framing — recovery/readiness scoring — is what Fitbod and Arvo ship, and it is the wrong fork here: it needs a fatigue model this V1 has correctly excluded, and the recurring critique of it is that soreness is not readiness. Arvo’s fourth state, untrained, is the most interesting mechanic found, because it turns a map from “what I did” into “what I’m neglecting” with no model attached. The genre’s dominant failure mode is being decorative — pretty, then never opened again, because the map has no next action. Two gaps are open here specifically: nobody surfaces anterior/posterior as the front/back toggle itself, and almost nobody offers an all-time map outside a paid tier.

Past learnings

There is no docs/solutions/ store yet. The prior Workout-tab ideation from the same week established modifiers that apply app-wide — actions in the thumb arc, the streak may not use the accent orange, no congratulatory copy, a quick-log fast path matters — and rejected a grace-day meter, a “most overdue muscle” callout, lifetime counters, and the full-bleed diagram as a cross-tab start control. ADR 0003 accepted that reaching another tab from the deepest Muscles screen costs four back-presses.

Two forks to settle before any of this is built. Ideas 1 and 15 are incompatible landing compositions — one puts a sub-group list beside the diagram, the other removes everything but the diagram. And idea 7 keeps the front/back buttons in order to hang a number on them, which rules out replacing those buttons with a drag gesture (cut, below). Pick one of each; they are presented together because the choice is real, not because both can ship.

1. Collapse the browse chain into one landing

Axis: the browse drill-down Confidence 92% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionPut the 19 sub-group rows as a companion list directly beneath the body diagram on the landing. A segment tap or a row tap goes straight to that sub-group’s exercise list. Delete docs/03’s separate “Muscle group selection” and “Sub-muscle group selection” screens entirely. Handle the single genuinely ambiguous segment — front-delt, which also carries side-delt on the front view — with a two-row bottom sheet on that one segment.

Basisdirect: docs/03 lists screens 2 and 3 separately, while line 104 of the same file says a segment tap “should go directly into that sub-muscle group’s exercise list, not into a generic parent-muscle view first.” Verified in muscle_taxonomy.dart: shoulders/front-delt and shoulders/side-delt both map to (front, shoulders/front-delt), and every other sub-group’s segment list is unique — exactly one collision in 42 segments. ADR 0003 records the chain as five deep, costing four back-presses to leave the tab.

RationaleThe spec contradiction is not a judgement call once you count: screen 3 exists to disambiguate one tap target out of 42. Resolving it by deletion rather than by picking a side removes two screens from the build and shortens the app’s most back-press-expensive chain. The companion list is not an accessibility afterthought either — docs/00 §13 records that there is no side-delt artwork, so a sub-group with 14 primary exercises has no tap target of its own on any view. Without a list path, part of the taxonomy is unreachable by tap.

DownsidesOverrides a written spec, so docs/03 needs updating rather than quietly diverging. A diagram plus 19 rows is a longer landing than the prototype shows, which pushes the diagram up and may make it feel smaller. The bottom sheet is a one-off interaction that exists for a single segment, which is the kind of special case that tends to rot.

2. The landing fill answers absence, and is windowed

Axis: the landing Confidence 85% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionMake the landing diagram’s fills answer “what have I not touched” rather than “what have I done.” A segment fills when it was trained inside the current window and stays muted when it was not, so the screen’s visual subject becomes the dark regions. The window is a steppable calendar week with left/right arrows, plus a named “all time” option — not a lifetime default.

Basisexternal: Arvo’s recovery map carries “untrained” as a first-class fourth state, and no surveyed app does neglect well inside a non-recovery map. Hevy defaults to the current calendar week and steps it with arrows. direct: muscles_root.dart states the rule this must satisfy: “This tab has no empty state… only the diagram’s fills differ.”

RationaleThe window is not a nicety, it is what keeps the promise in muscles_root.dart survivable. A binary trained/untrained map with no window is uniformly lit for a 300-session user — the screen degrades to decoration exactly for the people who earned it, which is the genre’s documented failure mode. Framing the fill around absence gives the map a question to answer without a fatigue model, a readiness score, or a recommendation, all of which are out of scope. It is performedOn arithmetic and stores nothing.

DownsidesA window control is the only stateful element on an otherwise stateless screen, and the default choice quietly decides what the tab means on first open. “Neglect” framing risks reading as nagging, which the prior Workout-tab ideation already rejected once in the form of a most-overdue-muscle callout — the difference has to stay in the copy and the absence of any verdict. Depends on the Session/SetEntry tables, so it cannot ship first.

3. The landing map counts primary muscles only

Axis: your own data in a reference tab Confidence 90% Complexity: Low

DescriptionWhen the landing diagram sums across many sessions, count primary muscles only and ignore secondary. Keep the two-tone primary/secondary fill on the exercise detail diagram, where it describes a single movement rather than a summed history. One rule, two screens, deliberately opposite answers.

Basisdirect: computed over all 260 entries in exercises.json and independently recomputed during verification — secondary appearances run shoulders/front-delt 43, forearms 41, triceps 41, back/upper 32, abs/upper 27, against calves 4, quads 3 and chest/upper 0; 72 exercises carry no secondary array at all. external: secondary attribution is named in the research as the credibility cliff — under-count and users notice neglected arms, over-count and every muscle glows and the map says nothing.

RationaleThis is the single decision most likely to make the map worthless, and the shipped dataset already answers it. The distribution is not close to balanced: count secondaries and front-delt, forearms and triceps glow permanently regardless of what was actually trained, while chest/upper can never light from them at all. It also settles the direct-1.0 / secondary-0.5 counting convention the research describes as the serious-user expectation — on this dataset it would be actively misleading.

DownsidesUsers who do a lot of compound work will see arms stay dark despite real indirect volume, which is the exact complaint levelled at COROS for primary-only attribution. The asymmetry between the two screens is defensible but non-obvious, so someone will eventually “fix” it into consistency unless the reason is written down.

4. One search field, three vocabularies

Axis: finding a specific exercise Confidence 85% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionIndex the 19 sub-group labels and the 8 equipment values alongside the 260 exercise names, in the one search field. Typing “lats” lands on that sub-group’s exercise list; “kettlebell” lands on the kettlebell list; “swing” lands on the exercise. Each result is labelled by kind so the three never blur together.

Basisdirect: docs/03 line 11 scopes search to exercise names only — “Typing an exercise name (e.g. ‘barbell curl’) jumps straight to that exercise’s detail page if there’s a clear match, or to a short filtered list if the query is ambiguous.” muscle_taxonomy.dart already carries 19 human-readable sub-group labels, and exercises.json carries 8 distinct equipment values — 27 extra strings, no new data.

RationaleThe tab’s stated identity is MuscleWiki, yet the only place a muscle can be named is a diagram tap. A user who already knows they want lats has to abandon the search bar and go hunting on a silhouette. Indexing the other two vocabularies makes the field a complete substitute for the diagram rather than a parallel path — which matters most for exactly the sub-groups the artwork handles badly. Equipment is the constraint a user actually faces standing in a gym, and docs/03 already treats it as first-class by putting an equipment chip on every exercise row.

DownsidesThree result kinds in one list needs a clear visual grammar or it reads as noise, and it complicates the “clear match jumps straight to detail” rule — a muscle match should presumably never auto-jump. Adding a second navigation axis to a tab named Muscles is a framing decision, not a search tweak.

5. Shared components, and session state read ambiently

Axis: the browse drill-down Confidence 88% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionBuild one MuscleDiagram widget parameterised by view, mode and fill-map, with the Path objects for all four assets cached once in a provider. Build one accordion list component shared with the Workout tab’s template muscle list. And have the exercise detail screen read the active session’s state (none / ad-hoc / template) from a provider rather than receiving it as a route argument.

Basisdirect: docs/03 already asserts both shapes — the diagram is “one reusable full-body component used on every screen,” interactive on landing and sub-group, read-only on detail; and the sub-muscle expansion is “the same accordion pattern as the Workout tab.” body_paths.dart’s own header instructs callers to cache built Path objects rather than rebuild them per frame. docs/03 gives the three-state visibility table for “Add to current workout.”

RationaleThe taxonomy has sharp edges — side-delt maps to front-delt on the front view only, traps live under back/upper, serratus renders as a second path element routed to obliques. If four screens each re-implement segment lookup, those exceptions get fixed in one screen and silently missed in another. The ambient-session half matters because exercise detail is reachable from at least three places with three different session states; threading it as a route argument means every call site must remember to forward it, and a future fourth entry point gets it wrong by default.

DownsidesThis is architecture, not a user-visible feature, so it competes for time against things a user can see. A widget parameterised for four call sites tends to accumulate flags; the accordion in particular may already exist in flight on the Workout tab, which decides who extracts from whom rather than whether to share.

6. History-first ranking in a shared matcher core

Axis: finding a specific exercise Confidence 80% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionWrite one matching-and-ranking function over exerciseLibraryProvider — exercises you have logged before first (most recent first), then name prefix, then name substring, then muscle and equipment matches — and consume it from both the Muscles-tab reference search and the Workout tab’s ad-hoc search. The two differ only in where a tap goes: exercise detail here, straight to set-logging there. A query that matches no exercise name falls through to muscle and equipment matches instead of returning an empty state.

Basisdirect: exercise_search_screen.dart is a placeholder carrying a TODO for build-order step 4, so the ranking is genuinely unwritten; its own comment states that the ad-hoc path must route to set-logging rather than through detail, “because routing every logging action through detail taxes the majority who already know the lift.” reasoned: the app is local-only with no telemetry, so the three signals modern search leans on — popularity, click-through, editorial curation — are all unavailable. What remains is lexical match over 260 names, a fixed 19-node classification, and the user’s own log. Ranking by the log is the only genuinely personal signal available, and it is derived on read by construction, with no recents list to store or invalidate.

RationaleWith 260 exercises and a four-character query, the best result is almost always one of the handful the user actually does. Deciding the tiers once means the two entry points cannot drift on typo tolerance or on what counts as a clear match, and both unbuilt screens can ship from one piece of work rather than two.

DownsidesHistory-first ranking is invisible on day one and only earns its keep after a few weeks of logging, so it will look like over-engineering at review time. It also lets personal data reorder results in a tab specified as pure reference, which is the same philosophical question idea 13 raises — worth answering once for both.

7. The front/back flip is the balance readout

Axis: the landing Confidence 75% Complexity: Low

DescriptionHang a derived anterior/posterior number on the two flip controls the landing already has — for instance Front 62 and Back 38 for the current window — so that flipping between views is a comparison rather than a camera move. No new screen, no ratio verdict, no statement that any particular split is correct.

Basisexternal: the research names this as an open gap — every push/pull balance feature found in prior art is a separate screen with its own ratio computation (JEFIT ships a Movement Balance Engine; Strength Balance is a standalone app), and nobody surfaces anterior/posterior as the front/back toggle itself. This app already has both views and a flip control.

RationaleThe two halves of the comparison already exist as two rendered assets and one control, so the feature is mostly a refusal to build anything extra. It also answers the “decorative by default” critique in the mildest possible way: a ledger states, it does not accuse — which keeps it clear of the most-overdue-muscle callout the prior ideation rejected.

DownsidesIt reassigns meaning to an existing control, which changes its label and probably its size. A raw set count invites a correctness reading the app is deliberately refusing to supply — users will ask what the right ratio is. And it forecloses replacing those buttons with a drag-to-turn gesture, which was the competing proposal.

8. An “equivalent options” rail on the exercise page

Axis: the exercise page Confidence 78% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionClose every exercise page with a short rail of substitutes that share the same primary sub-group, ordered by equipment distance and matching loadType where possible. It answers “the bench is taken” and “is there a better one for this muscle” without a recommendation engine, a score, or a generated word of prose.

Basisdirect: every field the substitution needs already ships — a record carries equipment, loadType, primary[] and secondary[]. There is real choice to surface: glutes has 26 primary exercises, quads 25, back/upper 23. reasoned: the tab exists for a user who “doesn’t want to build a full workout and just wants to find or understand a specific exercise” — and the question behind that is usually comparative, not singular. A mechanical equivalence (same primary muscle, different equipment) makes no claim the app has to stand behind, which keeps it clear of the AI and recommendation exclusions.

RationaleIt is the strongest available answer to the genre’s no-next-action critique that stays inside V1’s boundaries. It also converts the deepest screen in the chain from a dead end into a lateral hop, so the four-back-press cost ADR 0003 accepted is incurred less often — the user stops needing to go back up at all.

Downsides“Equivalent” is a stronger word than the data supports — sharing a primary sub-group does not make a machine press equivalent to a barbell bench press, and the wording will need care to avoid implying the app endorses the swap. It also touches the app’s most-shared screen, so it lands on the set-logging entry point too.

9. Exercise page ordering branches on whether history exists

Axis: the exercise page Confidence 80% Complexity: Low

DescriptionOne screen, one formatter, two orderings. For an exercise the user has never logged, the reference content leads — diagram, chips, and the four prose fields — because that is the only reason the page was opened. For one they have logged, the Best/Last/Times tiles and the recent log lead, because they came to check what to beat.

Basisdirect: docs/03 already branches this screen on history presence — when nothing is logged it replaces “the entire history block — all three tiles and the recent list — with a single muted line… Do not render three tiles of dashes.” And it justifies the tile set on the same grounds: “the last session is what the user is trying to beat, an average is trivia.” Verification confirmed the doc branches on content substitution only, not on ordering — so this is a genuine addition rather than a restatement.

RationaleThe spec already accepted that this screen serves two populations; it just stopped short of letting that change the layout. Two orderings cost almost nothing on top of a branch that has to exist anyway, and they remove a scroll from the most common return visit — checking last time’s numbers on a lift you do every week.

DownsidesTwo layouts for one screen is a thing teams either endorse once or forbid once; decided mid-build it becomes an inconsistency. It also means a user’s first and tenth visit to the same page look different, which can be disorienting the first time it flips.

10. Collapse the reviewed prose; open Common mistakes

Axis: the exercise page Confidence 75% Complexity: Low

DescriptionRender the four reviewed fields as labelled collapsible rows — Setup, Posture, Execution, Common mistakes — verbatim, with Common mistakes expanded by default and the other three collapsed. Not one word changes; only placement does.

Basisdirect: CLAUDE.md is explicit that “Exercise content carries injury risk. Do not rewrite, summarise, or regenerate the setup / posture / execution / commonMistakes fields. They are reviewed copy.” The fields average roughly 76 words each across the library, which makes the page a wall of text if all four render open.

RationaleThis is the only way to shorten the page without touching a reviewed word. It also turns an accident into a decision: as specified, which safety content the user sees first is determined by layout order, and nobody chose that. Defaulting the injury-relevant field open makes the choice deliberate.

DownsidesCollapsed setup and posture content is content people will not read, and for a genuinely unfamiliar lift those matter as much as the mistakes list. Deciding which single field is default-visible is a judgement about injury risk that probably should not be made by whoever writes the widget.

11. Lateral navigation instead of back-presses

Axis: the browse drill-down Confidence 72% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionCarry a small diagram into the exercise-list screen with the current sub-group lit and every other segment live. Tapping a neighbour replaces the route rather than pushing, so the stack never grows and the user re-orients without a screen transition. Optionally the same sideways move from the pinned title, as a sibling picker.

Basisdirect: ADR 0003 records that “the browse chain is five screens deep… reaching another tab from the bottom of it takes four back presses,” and accepted that cost rather than solving it. docs/03 already requires the reusable diagram component on this screen, so the widget is not new. AppScreen.pushed exposes titleWidget and actions, which is where a sibling picker would live.

RationaleThe expensive gesture in a reference tab is not going deeper, it is going sideways — comparing lats against upper back today means pop, pop, tap, tap. Replace-instead-of-push makes that cost never accrue, and it does so without touching the navigation model the ADR settled: no per-tab back stack is ever observed, and the change is entirely intra-tab.

DownsidesReplace-instead-of-push changes the navigation contract for a chain an ADR already reasoned about, so it should be recorded against that ADR rather than slipped in. A second diagram on a list screen competes for vertical space with the list itself, and a map that navigates differently here than on the landing is a subtle inconsistency.

12. Decide whether the assisted inversion is user-facing at all

Axis: the exercise page Confidence 70% Complexity: Low

DescriptionTwo defensible routes, and the spec cannot hold both. Either carry the inversion on the value — a permanent direction marker in the Best tile, or a relabelled tile such as “Lightest assist” — so the correction cannot be separated from the number by scrolling or a screenshot. Or take the spec’s own hint and remove the user-facing signalling entirely, letting the label do all the work with no banner. What should not happen is shipping a banner nobody reads above a number that looks wrong.

Basisdirect: CLAUDE.md calls this out as “the only place in the app where a smaller number is the record, and it is the thing most likely to ship backwards.” docs/03 currently mitigates it with a single banner — “Less assistance is better. Your record is the lightest assist you’ve used.” — and adds, in its own words, that if a second such banner ever becomes necessary “that is a signal the inversion shouldn’t be user-facing at all.” Only 3 of 260 exercises are assisted.

RationaleThree exercises is exactly why this ships backwards: too rare for everyday use to catch a sign error, too rare for anyone to remember the banner. Putting the comparator on the value makes the tile self-describing, so a wrong comparison reads as visibly wrong in a screenshot and a widget test has something to assert on other than banner presence. The spec text already reads as an invitation to reconsider, so the decision is open rather than settled.

DownsidesSpecial-casing one tile for 3 of 260 exercises adds a branch to a screen that is otherwise uniform across four load types. Relabelling the tile breaks the Best/Last/Times symmetry that makes the three-tile row scannable. And whichever route is chosen, the doc needs amending rather than accumulating a second mitigation on top of the first.

13. A derived training-frequency line

Axis: your own data in a reference tab Confidence 68% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionOn the sub-group or exercise-list screen, show a compact derived line counting how often that sub-group appears across recent logged sessions — “trained in 6 of your last 10 sessions.” Computed from performedOn alone, on read, with no fatigue model and no stored counter.

Basisexternal: clinical pain body maps are the closest structural match found — grid-cell templates where each region is binary per session, which makes a frequency map (how often a region is marked, across time) trivially computable. This app’s 42 fixed segments are exactly that grid-based family, so the frequency-of-training map is the direct analogue, and it needs no model. direct: the derived-on-read rule already covers the trained tick, so this adds a bucket to an existing computation rather than a new kind of value.

RationaleIt gives a reference screen a next action without crossing into recovery or readiness, which the research is clear is the wrong fork for an offline, no-AI V1 — soreness tracks novelty, not adaptation, and a readiness score that cannot show the load that produced it reads as decoration. A frequency count is traceable by construction: it is a count of sessions the user can go and look at.

Downsides“6 of your last 10” is a ratio a user will want to compare against something, and the app deliberately offers no target — the credible volume band (roughly 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week) is not settled enough per muscle to hard-code without sounding authoritative. Depends on the Session tables. And it is more history bleeding into a tab specified as pure reference.

14. An equipment coverage map

Axis: the landing Confidence 65% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionLet the user declare the equipment they actually have, as a preference in the existing Settings table, then dim the sub-groups their kit cannot reach and show a count for the ones it can. A coverage map, in the sense a utility or mobile network publishes one: the same map, dimmed where service does not reach.

Basisdirect: computed and independently re-verified over exercises.json — filtering to the 47 bodyweight exercises, the only sub-groups with zero primary coverage are biceps/biceps, shoulders/rear-delt and shoulders/side-delt; 16 of 19 sub-groups remain covered. Equipment has 8 distinct values. The Settings table is explicitly for preferences, never derived values, so an owned-equipment list belongs there.

RationaleThis is the only personalisation in the tab that needs no session data at all, so it ships before the Session tables land and works on first launch. It also honours the rule in muscles_root.dart that a zero-session user must see a meaningful screen — here they see one tailored to their actual gym rather than a uniformly muted silhouette.

DownsidesIt introduces a persistent preference into a tab that currently has none, and that preference wants to be respected by search and the ad-hoc flow too, so the blast radius is larger than the landing. A user whose kit changes, or who trains at two gyms, is poorly served by a single list. And dimming reachable muscles risks reading as “you cannot train this,” which is rarely literally true.

15. Full-bleed diagram — the alternative landing

Axis: the landing Confidence 55% Complexity: Medium

DescriptionThe opposite composition to idea 1. Remove the search field, the flip buttons and the hint copy from the body entirely and let the map occupy the whole scrollable. Search becomes a magnifier in the pinned header via the unused actions slot; front/back becomes a horizontal swipe on the map, matching the tab-bar swipe idiom already shipped. The tab becomes one object rather than a page of controls.

Basisdirect: verified that L0TabSpec in l0_shell.dart declares final List<Widget>? actions documented as header controls a tab adds before the profile avatar, and that none of the three tabs currently passes it — the slot exists and is unused. The tab-swipe idiom shipped in commit 0bcb95c. The prior Workout-tab ideation did reject a full-bleed diagram, but verification confirmed it rejected the diagram as a cross-tab start control, which is a different question from the Muscles tab’s own layout.

RationaleOn the Workout tab the diagram is a side effect of logging; here it is the content, so the earlier rejection does not automatically transfer. Naming that explicitly is worth more than inheriting the veto by accident. If the diagram really is the tab, giving it the whole screen is the honest expression of that.

DownsidesThis is the weakest-confidence idea here, and it is directly incompatible with idea 1: a full-bleed map has no room for a companion list, so it reinstates the tap-precision problem that the side-delt artwork gap makes concrete. Hiding search behind a header icon demotes the faster of the tab’s two entry paths. It is included because the fork deserves stating, not because it is recommended over idea 1.
